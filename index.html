Calories, roughly.
Weight, precisely.

Ballpark Figure is a scale, made useful.

Your weight moves a kilogram or more in a day on water alone. Ballpark runs your scale readings through a Kalman filter and a smoother — the estimator used for tracking noisy signals — and draws the line underneath, with the rate of change it implies.

It logs food too, deliberately roughly. For each meal you tick a portion — small, medium or large, with a nudge either way for a tiny or a huge one — or none, if you skipped it. Snacks add up rather than replacing each other. That is the whole input. No barcode scanner, no food database, no photograph analysed by a model that returns “642 kcal” as though it had measured something.

What it does

A trend line, not yesterday’s number

Over a fortnight of ordinary living, roughly 84% of a unit change in body weight is fat-free mass — water, gut contents, glycogen. The filter separates the signal from that, and the app shows you both: the raw readings as points, the trend as a line.

A food log that admits it is guessing

Three portions per meal, calibrated once to what your own medium portion is worth. Three meals, or up to six if that is how you eat. A meal you skipped is one tap, and it is different from a meal you forgot — the app needs to know which.

What you actually spend

Ballpark works out your energy expenditure from your own logged intake against what the scale did, rather than from a formula off your age and height. The figure is there from day one, as an estimate from your body weight; it becomes provisional as complete days build up, and measured once your own data carries it — with its uncertainty printed beside it at every stage. It is in the units of your own logging: if your portions run low, the figure runs low with them, and the two cancel when you log against it. The app says so, in those words, on the card itself.

A goal, if you want one

State a goal weight and the app will tell you how far there is to go and how long your own measured rate would take to get there. It never invents a goal, never adjusts one you set, and never treats missing it as anything at all. Without a goal you still get one plain sentence at the top of the screen every time you open it — “Down 0.4 kg over the last month” — rather than five statistics and no verdict.

Log without opening it

Thirteen widgets for the Home and Lock Screen, including one for the maintenance figure, one that records a weigh-in from the home screen, and one that adds a hundred calories from the Lock Screen. Four Control Centre buttons. And Siri: “log my weight”, “log a medium meal”. Tapping a portion on a widget writes straight to the log without the app ever opening.

Your data, and the door out of here

Export everything as two CSV files whenever you like. The weights file carries the filter’s own trend and slope beside each reading, so the maths can be checked without re-running it. A local-only app owes you an exit more than a syncing one does, not less: there is no copy on a server to fall back on.

Free, and staying that way

Every number the app shows, every meal and every widget is free. If Ballpark is useful to you, you can support it with a one-time purchase, at whichever of three prices suits you — all three do the same thing. As a thank-you, it unlocks extra colour schemes and app icons. Nothing else.

What it will not do

  • No account, no sign-in, no server. Syncing between your own devices goes through your own iCloud, where the developer cannot read it; switch iCloud off and the app works exactly the same on one device. The one link out — to Open Food Facts, for looking up what something is worth — hands a web address to Safari and carries nothing about your log with it.
  • No advertising, no analytics, no crash reporting, no third-party SDKs.
  • It never writes to Apple Health — it only reads weight, if you allow it.
  • It will not nag you to log food. A guess made to satisfy a notification is worse than no guess.

Support

The maintenance figure still says “estimated” or “provisional”

It starts as an estimate from your body weight, turns provisional after three complete days, and is marked measured once it rests on twenty-one complete days and four weeks of weighing. A complete day is one where every meal your own schedule expects is logged, including the ones you mark none because you skipped them. Half-logged days are left out entirely rather than counted light, because a day missing its dinner looks exactly like a day with a large deficit.

My weight from my scale is not showing up

Readings from Apple Health arrive by themselves when you open the app, and usually in the background too, though iOS decides when that runs. Pull down on the Weight screen to check straight away. If nothing arrives, check Settings › Health › Data Access & Devices › Ballpark and make sure reading weight is allowed. Manual entries always win over Health ones, so a reading you corrected by hand is never overwritten by a later import.

I recalibrated a meal and my history looks wrong

When you set a new “medium” portion, the app offers to re-price the last fourteen days of meals that were logged with a bucket. Meals where you typed a number are left alone. If you declined at the time, set the medium portion again and accept the offer.

I bought the supporter purchase on another device

It follows your Apple Account, and Family Sharing, by itself. If the extra colour schemes do not appear, open Settings › Support Ballpark and tap Restore a previous purchase.

The numbers look small on my phone

Explanatory text follows your system text size (Settings › Display & Brightness › Text Size), in the app and in the widgets. The numbers and the tap targets are fixed by design, and the whole layout scales up a little on larger phones.

How do I get my data out?

Settings › Your data gives you two CSV files — weights and meals — through the normal share sheet. Nothing is uploaded; you choose where the copy goes.

Why does it want me to mark meals I skipped?

Because a meal you skipped and a meal you forgot look identical otherwise, and the difference is the largest source of error in the maintenance figure. A day missing its dinner looks exactly like a day with a large deficit. Only days where every meal is accounted for are used.

Privacy policy

Last updated: 24 September 2026.

Ballpark Figure is a weight tracker with a simple food log. The short version: your data is yours, it syncs only through your own iCloud account, and the developer has no server and cannot read any of it.

What the app collects

Nothing is collected by us. There is no account to create, no sign-in, no analytics, no advertising, no crash reporting, and no third-party software development kits of any kind. There is no server operated by the developer, and no copy of your data that the developer can reach. The app does make network requests, for exactly one purpose: keeping your own devices in step through iCloud.

iCloud sync

If you are signed in to iCloud, the app keeps your weights, meals, snacks and measurements in step across your own devices using CloudKit’s private database. The data goes to your iCloud account and counts against your iCloud storage. The developer cannot read it — a private database is not visible to the app’s author through the CloudKit dashboard or any API, which is enforced by Apple’s infrastructure rather than by a promise on this page. Apple operates the storage and their own privacy policy governs it.

Your settings — portion calibrations, goal, units, colour scheme — are kept in step the same way, through iCloud’s key-value storage, which is also held in your own account.

If you are not signed in, or you turn iCloud off for this app in Settings › [your name] › iCloud, everything stays on the device alone and the app works exactly as before.

What the app stores on your device

All of the following stays in the app’s own storage on your iPhone, and in the shared storage the app uses for its widgets:

  • Weight readings — the value, the date, and whether you typed it or it came from Apple Health.
  • Meals and snacks — which portion you tapped, the resulting calorie estimate, and when you logged it.
  • Your settings — portion calibrations, day patterns, daily target, goal weight, reminder time, and unit preference.
  • Exports you ask for — a CSV you request is written to the device's temporary storage and handed to the system share sheet. Where it goes next is your choice, and the app plays no part in it.

Apple Health

If you grant permission, the app reads body-mass (weight) samples from Apple Health so readings from a connected scale appear without retyping.

The app never writes to Apple Health. This is enforced two ways: there is no write method anywhere in the code, and authorisation is requested with an empty “share” set — requestAuthorization(toShare: [], read: [bodyMass]) — so iOS never offers write permission and never grants it.

The Info.plist does carry NSHealthUpdateUsageDescription, and until 20 September 2026 this page said it deliberately did not. That was true, and Apple’s upload validation rejects it: a writing purpose string is required of every app holding the HealthKit entitlement, whether or not the app writes. The string is there, it says the app does not write, and it is never shown to you, because no write authorisation is ever requested.

Health data read by the app is copied into the app’s own local storage so the app still works if you deny or revoke permission. With your permission, iOS may wake the app briefly in the background when Health gains a new weight, so your widgets stay current; that reading, too, stays on the device and in your own iCloud. It is never transmitted anywhere else. You can revoke access at any time in Settings › Health › Data Access & Devices, or in Settings › Privacy & Security › Health.

Camera and photo library

The app does not use the camera and cannot read your photo library. It requests no permission for either. An earlier version photographed meals; that feature was removed, and the app deletes any photographs left behind by it on first launch.

Links to other websites

The app links to Open Food Facts, an open, community-run food database, for looking up what something is worth. Opening it hands a web address to Safari; nothing about your log goes with the link. Once there you are subject to that site’s privacy policy, not this one.

Purchases

The optional supporter purchase is sold and processed by Apple through the App Store. The developer receives no payment details and no information about who bought it; the app checks whether it has been bought on the device itself, through Apple’s StoreKit.

Notifications

If you switch on the weigh-in reminder, the app schedules a local notification on your device. Nothing is sent through any server.

Sharing

Your information is not sold, shared, or disclosed to anyone. The only party that handles it besides you is Apple, as the operator of the iCloud account it syncs through. There are no advertisers, no analytics providers and no data brokers involved in the operation of this app, and there is nothing for the developer to sell, because the developer has no copy.

Deleting your data

Deleting the app removes the copy on that device. If iCloud sync is on, the copy in your iCloud account is not removed by deleting the app — remove it in Settings › [your name] › iCloud › Manage Account Storage. Within the app you can delete individual weight entries, meals, snacks and measurements, and a deletion syncs to your other devices like any other change. Data the app read from Apple Health is not deleted from Apple Health by deleting the app — manage that in the Health app.

Children

The app is not directed at children and collects nothing from anyone.
